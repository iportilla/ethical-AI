# Classroom Exercise: Three Cases, One Question

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Format:** small groups (4–5 students) · **No laptops or code required**

All three fall 2026 cases come down to the same question: **when an AI system or AI company gets into trouble, who is responsible for catching it, and were they in the room?** This exercise has students compare the cases side by side before (or after) the full labs.

| Case | One-line summary | Lab |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nvidia** | The CEO resigns after a hidden relationship tied to foreign intelligence; accountability rested with one person. | [Lab 1](lab1-nvidia-accountability.md) |
| **Microsoft** | A research group is folded into a "scaling, not research" org, AutoGen is archived, and a 27-hour Outlook outage hits during mass layoffs. | [Lab 2](lab2-research-vs-scale.md) |
| **Gated models** | Frontier cyber models go to governments and approved partners first, while AI shrinks the time to exploit a patch to under a day. | [Lab 3](lab3-gated-models-patch-window.md) |

## Setup (5 min)

Split the class into groups and assign each group one case as its **home case**. Hand out the one-page case summary (the "Case summary" section of each lab). Every group should also skim the other two summaries.

## Round 1: Who was supposed to catch it? (15 min)

For your home case, fill in this table on a whiteboard or shared sheet:

| Question | Your answer |
| --- | --- |
| What went wrong, or what risk is at stake? | |
| Which **Microsoft principle** is most at stake? Which **IBM pillar**? | |
| Who was responsible for catching or preventing it (a role, not a name)? | |
| Were they present, empowered and independent? | |
| What is **known** versus **suspected**? List one of each. | |

> **Evidence check:** each case has claims that circulated widely but are not proven, such as a third affair (Lab 1), layoffs causing the outage (Lab 2), and open weights making gating pointless (Lab 3). Label these clearly as suspected.

## Round 2: Gallery walk (10 min)

Groups rotate to the other two tables, read them, and leave **one sticky note each**: either a challenge ("you assumed X") or a connection ("this is the same failure as our case because…").

## Round 3: Find the common pattern (10 min)

Back at the home table, the group picks the pattern it thinks best links all three cases and defends it in two sentences. Possible patterns (or come up with your own):

- **Single point of failure:** responsibility rests on one person or one team, and nobody covers when they're gone.
- **Speed over review:** scaling, shipping or releasing faster than oversight can keep up.
- **Gatekeeping without accountability:** someone decides who gets access or protection, but nobody audits the decision.
- **Lost institutional knowledge:** the people who understood the system are reorganized, laid off or never consulted.

## Wrap-up: One rule (10 min)

Each group proposes **one governance rule** that would have helped in all three cases (for example "no AI system or org change ships without a named, independent reviewer who can say no"). The class votes on the strongest rule. The instructor closes by contrasting it with IBM's model, in which research initiatives report into a company-wide AI Ethics Board.

## Exit ticket (individual, 2 min)

On an index card: *Which case changed your mind about something, and what was it?*

## Instructor notes

- The exercise works as a **preview** (before the labs, to frame them) or as a **synthesis** (after, using students' own lab results as evidence).
- Grade participation only. The goal is comparison and evidence discipline, not right answers.
- Recheck the case facts the morning of class; all three stories are still developing.
